## Part One: The Wrong Kind of Warming

### Chapter 1: Forty-One Quakes

At 3:12 a.m. on a Tuesday, the seismograph at the Hollis Caldera Observatory drew a line so jagged that Dr. Tomás Reyes assumed the machine had a stroke.

It had not. He ran the diagnostics twice, made a pot of coffee in the observatory's one working mug, and watched the line do it again. By dawn he had counted forty-one quakes under the Wyoming–Idaho border, none above magnitude 3, all of them shallow, and all of them stacked along a ring that matched, almost to the kilometer, the outline of the ancient magma chamber the National Park Service called "a geologic feature of interest" on its brochures.

He sent the data to Washington. Washington sent back a form.

By noon he had spoken to a deputy assistant, a deputy to the assistant, and a woman named Brenda who said the word "alarmist" with the warmth of a paper cut. The final call came from Commissioner Helen Ostrander of the Federal Geohazard Review Office, a person who had never been wrong in public and did not plan to start.

"Dr. Reyes," she said, "I've reviewed your forty-one quakes. Last year Yellowstone gave us four thousand."

"Those weren't in a ring."

"Everything looks like a ring if you want it badly enough. Submit your findings through the proper channel."

"What's the proper channel?"

"I'll have someone send you the form."

Tomás hung up, looked out the window at the sagebrush, the tourists, the elk doing nothing in particular, and understood with perfect clarity that the world would end on a Tuesday because of paperwork.

---

Two thousand miles east, in a hotel ballroom in Denver, Dr. Nora Whitlock was being given a plaque for saving the planet.

It was a nice plaque. Walnut, brass plate, a small engraved globe that looked suspiciously like it was smiling. The Halvorsen Prize for Atmospheric Stewardship came every year to one scientist whose work had measurably bent the emissions curve, and this year the curve was her.

Twenty-two years. Sixty-one papers. One landmark model of sulfur and carbon feedback that had been cited in three treaties, two lawsuits, and a Senate hearing where a man in a very good suit had called her "a prophet of austerity," which she'd thought was fair. She had testified. She had marched. She had once spent a night in a county jail in Nevada because she'd locked herself to a bulldozer, and the bulldozer had not even been the right one.

She was forty-six years old and she had not taken a vacation since the Obama administration.

"Nora," said the moderator, a bright young man who had clearly been told to keep it moving, "a few words for the room?"

She walked to the podium. Four hundred people looked up at her with the soft, expectant faces of those who needed to be told they'd been right.

She opened her mouth to deliver the speech she'd given nineteen times. *We have the tools. We have the science. What we need now is the will.*

What came out was: "I'd like to thank my mother, my postdocs, and my knees."

The room laughed. She hadn't been joking.

She tried again. "What I've learned in two decades is that the atmosphere is the most honest thing we've ever tried to talk to. It doesn't respond to speeches. It responds to chemistry." She paused, because her phone was buzzing against the podium. "And chemistry, I'll say, has never once cared how we feel about it."

Polite applause. The buzzing continued. She glanced down.

Unknown number. Wyoming area code. A text, all lowercase, from someone who plainly did not text often:

*dr whitlock this is tomas reyes. your 2019 model on aerosol cooling. is it still right. please.*

She frowned. Her 2019 model was about *sulfate aerosols*, a footnote, a thing she'd modeled for the sake of completeness and never expected anyone to ask about. Aerosols were what happened when volcanoes erupted. They were what happened when the sky turned the color of dishwater and the summer didn't come.

She typed, one thumb, still smiling at four hundred people: *Yes. Why?*

The reply took eleven seconds, which is a long time when you are standing at a podium.

*because i think we have about six weeks*

The ballroom waited. The little brass globe on her plaque smiled up at her.

"Thank you," said Nora Whitlock, to the room, to the plaque, to the career she suddenly suspected had been a rehearsal. "I have to go."

### Chapter 2: The Fixer

Dex Calloway's office was a converted laundromat in Boulder, and he insisted this was a feature.

"People trust a place that used to clean things," he told Nora, kicking a chair toward her with one boot. "Subconscious. Also the rent's incredible."

He was fifty, tan in the way of men who had once worked outdoors and now only remembered it, with a salt-and-pepper mess of hair and the manner of a golden retriever who had gone to law school. Fourteen years ago he had been a plant engineer at a coal station in West Virginia. Eleven years ago the plant had been shut down by people like Nora. Ten years ago he had discovered he was better at explaining disasters than preventing them, and had founded Calloway Strategic Communications, whose entire business model was walking into rooms where something had gone horribly wrong and saying, *Okay, here's how we're going to talk about this.*

His clients included a pipeline company, a cruise line, a senator, and, once, a goat.

"The goat's a long story," he said. "Don't ask."

"I wasn't going to."

"Good. Tomás says you're the best aerosol person alive."

"Tomás is exaggerating."

"Tomás is a man who hasn't exaggerated since 1998. He's practically a ruler." Dex sat on the edge of a desk made from a repurposed dryer. "He also says you've seen his data."

She had. She'd flown to Wyoming that night, sat in a trailer that smelled of burnt coffee, and watched Tomás's forty-one quakes become sixty-three. She'd checked the ring. She'd checked the gas readings. She'd checked her own model, and her own model had checked back, and the conclusion was the sort of thing that made you want to fold up your whole life like a map.

The Hollis Caldera was going to erupt. Not might, not eventually. Within six weeks, give or take a fortnight and a lot of bad luck. When it did, the ash would be a catastrophe for a continent. But the ash was not the thing that frightened her.

The thing that frightened her was the sulfur.

"Walk me through it," Dex said. "Like I'm a client who's about to cry."

"A supereruption throws sulfur dioxide into the stratosphere," she said. "It turns into tiny droplets, sulfate aerosols, and they spread around the whole planet like a veil. They reflect sunlight. The surface cools. After Pinatubo in 1991, the Earth dropped half a degree for two years. Hollis is a thousand times bigger than Pinatubo."

"How cold are we talking?"

"Five to ten degrees, globally. For years. Maybe a decade." She watched his face. "Growing seasons collapse. Monsoons fail. Every major grain belt on Earth sees frost in July. Three billion people go hungry before the aerosols settle out."

Dex was quiet for what was, for Dex, a very long time.

"So the government's plan is—"

"The government's plan is a form."

"A *form.*" He laughed, one short, hopeless exhale. "God. I love it. Okay. What's *our* plan?"

Nora looked at the floor. The laundromat's old linoleum still had faint outlines where the machines had been bolted down.

"There isn't one. There are only mitigation papers, and every one of them says the same thing. You can't stop the eruption. You can only survive the winter."

"And how do you survive a winter?"

"You make it warmer."

The sentence sat between them like a dropped plate.

She had spent her entire career on the *other* half of that sentence. She'd built her name on the premise that warmer was the enemy. And now the only honest paragraph in her own model said that the planet was about to be dunked in ice water, and the responsible thing, the *scientific* thing, was to meet it with heat.

"Say that again," Dex said softly. "I want to hear how it sounds."

"We have to make it warmer."

He grinned. It was not a nice grin. It was the grin of a man who had been waiting fourteen years for the universe to admit it owed him one.

"Dr. Whitlock," he said, "I think you just ruined your life."

"Thank you."

"No, no, that's a *good* thing. That's the best possible starting position." He pulled a whiteboard out from behind the dryer. Someone had left a faded ghost of an old campaign on it: *WE'RE SORRY ABOUT THE GOAT.* He wiped it with his sleeve and uncapped a marker. "Because here's the thing about the end of the world. Nobody's going to want to hear it from the person who caused it, and nobody's going to want to hear it from the person who predicted it. They'll only believe it from a person they can't stand, telling them something they hate."

"That's an awful theory of communication."

"It's *my* awful theory of communication, and it works." He tapped the board. "I need a team. Small, quiet, off the books. Because the minute Ostrander finds out what we're planning, she's going to call it a crime."

"What *are* we planning?"

Dex wrote three words on the board and underlined them twice.

**MAKE IT WARMER.**

Below it, smaller, he added: *(Quietly.)*

"We start," he said, "with the things you hate most."

Nora closed her eyes. Somewhere in the back of her skull, nineteen years of protest signs shifted uneasily in their sleep.

"Start with the coal plant," she said.

### Chapter 3: The Gate

The Harlan Ridge Generating Station had been dead for six years, and it was not taking the news well.

It sat in a Pennsylvania valley like a cathedral someone had abandoned mid-sermon: four smokestacks, a rusting conveyor, a coal pile gone to weeds. The county had tried to turn it into a solar farm, a distribution center, and a haunted attraction. The haunted attraction had gone bankrupt. Even the ghosts had filed complaints.

Dex had leased it for a dollar a year from a township that was glad to see anyone.

"It's got the boilers," he said, rapping a knuckle on a pipe the width of a minivan. "It's got the turbines. It's got a rail spur, and it's got three hundred megawatts of potential that nobody's allowed to want."

"It has asbestos," said Nora.

"It has *character.*"

Behind them, in hard hats that did not match, stood the team. Dex had assembled it in nine days, which he called a personal best and Nora called a hostage situation.

There was Tomás, who had abandoned the observatory with two laptops and a thermos and now stood in the boiler hall looking like a man at his own surprise funeral. There was Odalys "Oz" Brandt, a chemical engineer who had spent thirty years running grout and slurry for mining companies and communicated mostly in grunts that Dex swore had meaning. There was Priya Venkataraman, a twenty-six-year-old logistics wizard who could reroute a freight train by sending one email, and who had joined, she said, because the alternative was a job at a startup that sold subscription socks. And there was Walt, who was seventy-one, who had run the control room at Harlan Ridge for thirty-four years, and who had shown up unasked on the first morning with his own lunchbox.

"Heard you're lighting her back up," Walt had said.

"How did you hear that?" Dex asked.

"I live four miles away. I've been listening to her not run for six years. It's like living next to a dog that stopped barking."

Now Walt stood at the control panel with his hand on a lever the size of a baseball bat, and he looked at Nora the way a priest looks at a person who has wandered into the wrong church but might stay.

"You're the one who hated her," he said.

"I hated what she did," said Nora.

"She didn't do it. We did it. She just burned what we gave her."

Nora said nothing. It was, she realized, the first thing anyone had said in a decade that she hadn't heard in a seminar.

---

The protesters arrived at 6:40 the next morning.

There were about a hundred and twenty of them, which in Nora's experience was the perfect number: large enough to be a story, small enough to be fed. They came up the access road in a ragged column with hand-painted banners and a papier-mâché polar bear on a hay cart. Someone had a drum. Someone had a ukulele. A woman at the front, tall and silver-haired in a green rain jacket, was carrying a bullhorn like a scepter.

Nora knew her instantly. It was Margaret Oyelaran, who had run the Appalachian Climate Alliance for twenty years and who had, once, years ago, at a wedding, taught Nora how to dance badly in public.

Margaret saw her. Her stride faltered for exactly one step.

Then she raised the bullhorn.

"SHUT IT DOWN," Margaret said, in the voice of a woman addressing a stranger, "OR WE CHAIN OURSELVES TO THIS GATE."

They did. It took four minutes. They had practiced.

Nora stood in the boiler-hall doorway with her hands clenched at her sides and watched twelve of her oldest friends lock themselves to the fence of the plant she was about to relight. One of them was Danny Kowalczyk, who had lost his job in 2018 for refusing to falsify a water report, and whose wife had made Nora a casserole when her father died.

Danny waved at her. A small, sad, unmistakable wave.

"I can handle this," Dex said at her elbow. "Let me handle this."

"You are not going to sell this to them."

"I'm not going to *sell* it. I'm going to *sit* with it." He rolled his shoulders like a man preparing to lift a piano. "Stay here. Watch the pro."

He walked down to the gate, hands in the air, smiling. She could not hear him. She could see him, though: the open palms, the slow nod, the way he crouched to Danny's level and listened. He listened for eleven minutes. She timed it. At one point Margaret said something short and cutting, and Dex took it, nodded, and said something back that made Margaret's mouth do a complicated thing.

When he returned, the grin was gone.

"What did you tell them?" Nora asked.

"The truth. Sort of."

"*Sort of?*"

"I said we were running a temporary emergency measure under federal contract, that there'd be public disclosure in six weeks, and that if I was lying, they could chain me to the smokestack personally." He rubbed his face. "I didn't tell them why. I couldn't. Not yet."

"That's not the truth."

"It's the *shape* of the truth. It buys us time." He glanced at the gate. "Margaret said, and I quote, 'Nora Whitlock would never do this.' And I said, 'You're right. The Nora you knew wouldn't.'"

It landed somewhere under her ribs.

"Was that necessary?"

"No," said Dex. "But it was accurate."

Behind them, from the control room, Walt's voice came over the old intercom, crackling, cheerful, enormous.

"BOILER TWO'S ABOUT TO LIGHT. ANYBODY WHO WANTS TO BE SOMEWHERE ELSE, NOW'S THE TIME."

Nora looked at the gate. Margaret was looking back at her, bullhorn lowered, face wide open with something worse than anger. Disappointment, probably. The kind you only get from people who once believed in you.

She thought about the sulfur. She thought about three billion people and a July frost. She thought about forty-one quakes that had become sixty-three, and a form, and a woman named Brenda.

Then she did the only thing she could think of, which was to walk to the control room, put her hand on the lever beside Walt's, and say, very quietly, "I'm so sorry," to the building, to the gate, to every tree she had ever signed a petition for.

"Don't apologize to her," said Walt, gently. "She's been waiting six years for someone to *ask.*"

They pulled the lever together.

Across the valley, a stack that had been silent since the spring of the Great Resignation coughed once, shuddered, and began, slowly, to breathe.

In the car park, a papier-mâché polar bear fell off its hay cart.

On the gate, Margaret Oyelaran began to cry, and it was the worst thing Nora had ever seen.
